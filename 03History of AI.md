# History of AI

The field of AI showed early promise. In the 1960s, Frank Rosenblatt created the  Perceptron, which was the first [[neural network]] that could learn. In 1966, the chatbot Eliza, gained attention and notoriety as a virtual therapist. Yet,  AI research was widely expensive and with no cohesive goal.  In 1972, Sir Richard Lighthill was tasked with reviewing the developing field of artificial intelligence to help direct funding decisions.  He defined the field not as a single line of inquiry, but as three distinct fields each with a different purpose in his paper Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey.

Category A was Advanced Automation, the grandfather of what we would now consider [[AI]]. This category’s chief goal was determining how machines could learn to replicate tasks traditionally undertaken by humans.  Category C was computer assisted CNS (central nervous system) studies. Category C , standing for computer-based Central Nervous System, defined as using computers to understand how our brains work. He specifically uses the terminology CNS instead of “brain” to highlight the focus on perception and movement - those preconscious and unconscious actions that happen within our biology - and not on the more human  “brainy” activities. Category B was robotics, or building robots, which, according to Lighthill, was at the time a questionable endeavor.  

After this report, the first AI winter began marked by a decline in funding for AI research. Another AI winter began in 1987 when the existing hardware could not keep up with the exponential number of possibilities available given a certain set of data.  Until just recently Most approached the problem of artificial intelligence from a reasoning, sequential understanding. They were all successful in some ways, but the idea of a machine being able to generate natural language or think like humans do was still out of reach. But in 2017, things were about to change.
